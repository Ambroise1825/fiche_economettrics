Lesson 1 · Chapter 0–1

Data vocabulary: cases, variables, samples

You'll be able toname each part of a dataset, tell a parameter from a statistic, and explain why we work on samples.

Every dataset in this course has the same shape: one row per case and one column per variable. A case is one individual, item, object or participant: a customer, a house, a car. A variable (also called a feature) is one attribute measured on each case: age, price, colour.

We almost never observe everyone. The population is the whole group we care about (all customers in France). The sample is the subset we actually measure (the 5,000 customers in customer_dbase). Inferential statistics means using the sample to say something about the population.

PopulationSample
A number that describes it is a…parameterstatistic
Meanµ (mu)x̄ (x-bar)
Standard deviationσ (sigma)s
Do we know it?Almost neverYes, we compute it
Rule of thumb: Greek letters are for the population (unknown), Latin letters are for the sample (known).

Why sample at all? A full census costs too much, some data are not available (a private profile), or they don't exist yet (not every house is on sale). The price of sampling is risk: a sample might not behave like the population, for example if you survey the children's table at a wedding. We accept a risk level, written α, usually 5%. In food safety or aerospace, 5% would be unacceptable.

Two ways to get a sample: a simple random sample gives every member of the frame (the list of the population) the same chance of being picked. A biased frame ruins everything: surveying tennis-club members to measure how much French people like tennis earns zero.

The research process (simplified)

  1. Initial question or observation
  2. Collect secondary data (papers and documents that already exist)
  3. Build a theory: a set of principles that explain behaviour and generate hypotheses. A theory must be testable.
  4. Break it into testable hypotheses
  5. Collect data that are accurate (correct value), valid (measures the right thing), reliable (sourced and reproducible) and relevant (fits the context)
  6. Analyse, conclude and report

Observing what the theory predicts does not prove it. You only fail to reject it. Newton looked "right" until 1905.

Variables by role

  • Independent variable (IV), also called explanatory variable or predictor: the assumed cause (surface, age of a house).
  • Dependent variable (DV), also called explained or outcome variable: the consequence (price). Usually there is one.
  • Extraneous variable: something you didn't take into account that may affect the result (tasting mayonnaise at 8 AM vs 8 PM).
  • Control variable: something you keep constant (same website design, only the colour changes).
  • Confounding (lurking) variable: a third variable that drives both the IV and the DV (weather drives both ice-cream and sunglasses sales).

Lesson 3 · Chapter 1

Describing one variable: centre, spread, box plot, outliers

You'll be able topick the right summary, read a box plot and compute the outlier fences.

Centre

Mean: the average. Median: the middle value of the ordered data (Q2). Mode: the most frequent value. The mean is sensitive to outliers and the median is resistant, so prefer the median when there are extreme values.

Example: 4, 7, 7, 9, 12 has mean 7.8 and median 7. Add one billionaire-style value, 60: the mean jumps to 16.5, but the median only moves to 8.

Position and spread

Quartiles: Q1 = 25th percentile, Q2 = median, Q3 = 75th percentile. The five-number summary is min, Q1, median, Q3, max. IQR = Q3 − Q1, the range of the middle 50%. The standard deviation s measures the typical distance to the mean. For a sample it divides by n − 1, which makes it an unbiased estimator; dividing by n would underestimate σ.

lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR   ·   upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQRA value outside the fences is a potential outlier: an unexpected, faulty or extreme value. SPSS draws it as a circle or star.

SPSS labels each outlier dot with its case number (the row), not its value. "Max value = 38" when 38 is a row number cost points in 2024.

Charts

Histogram: one quantitative variable grouped into classes. The bar height is the count (or %). Use it to see shape and outliers. Bar or pie chart: one qualitative variable, with a summary table of frequencies and percentages.

Two variables: scatter plots and the three correlations

You'll be able tochoose between Pearson, Spearman and Kendall, and read r.

With two quantitative variables, always draw a scatter plot first (explanatory variable on the x-axis). Read four things: direction (up or down), shape (linear or not), strength (how tightly the dots hug a line) and bivariate outliers.

SituationUse
2 quantitative, linear relationship, few outliers (roughly normal)Pearson's r (ρ in the population)
2 quantitative, monotonic but not linear (always rising, e.g. log or exponential)Spearman's rank correlation (works on ranks)
2 ordinal, or 1 ordinal + 1 quantitativeSpearman or Kendall's τ (Kendall suits small samples and many ties)

All three lie between −1 and +1. The sign gives the direction. Values near 0 mean no linear (Pearson) or monotonic (Spearman) link, though another kind of link may exist.

|r| < 0.4 weak · 0.4–0.6 moderate · > 0.6 strong

Anscombe's four datasets all have the same r, but Pearson is legitimate only for the first: the second is curved, the third has an outlier, and in the fourth X is constant except for one point. That is why you look at the plot.

Correlation does not imply causation. Look for a confounding variable (weather drives both ice cream and sunglasses), reverse causality, coincidence (spurious correlation) and data collected over time.

Pearson on two ordinal variables is wrong. The question "relationship between brand and colour" (two nominal variables) is not a correlation question at all: use a crosstab (lesson 5).

Lesson 6 · Chapter 2

Sample means vary: standard error and the Central Limit Theorem

You'll be able togive and use the formula of the standard deviation of the sample mean, and say why n matters.

Imagine drawing many samples of size n from the same wedding (N = 300 guests, µ = 40, σ = 10) and computing x̄ each time. You get a different x̄ each time. Those sample means form their own distribution, the sampling distribution of x̄.

σx̄ = σ / √n    estimated by    SE = s / √nThe standard deviation of the sample mean, also called the standard error. It is the spread between samples.
  • Larger n → smaller σx̄: big samples give means that cluster near µ.
  • Larger σ (individuals more spread out) → larger σx̄.
  • To halve the standard error you must quadruple n (because of the square root), so you get diminishing returns.
Don't mix up two standard deviations. s = spread of individuals inside one sample. s/√n = spread of sample means from sample to sample.

Central Limit Theorem: as n grows, the distribution of x̄ becomes normal, centred on µ, with standard deviation σ/√n, whatever the shape of the original variable. This is why we need large samples. With a small n we can only rely on normality if the variable itself is normal.

Estimator vs estimate

An estimator is a rule, defined before you see data: "take 10 random guests and average their ages". An estimate is the number that rule gives on one sample: "34". A good estimator is unbiased (no systematic error), consistent (better as n grows), efficient (most accurate for a given n) and robust (resists outliers). No estimator has all four.

Confidence intervals and the margin of error

You'll be able towrite the formula, explain each part's effect on the margin of error, and interpret an interval correctly.

A single number (x̄) is almost never exactly µ. A confidence interval gives a range we are confident contains µ:

x̄ ± tα/2, n−1 × s / √npoint estimate ± margin of error, where ME = critical value × standard error = tα/2, n−1 × s/√n. If σ is known (rare), use Zα/2 × σ/√n (Z = 1.96 at 95%).

We use Student's t instead of Z because σ is unknown and estimated by s. That extra uncertainty widens the interval. With more degrees of freedom (n − 1), t gets closer to Z: at 95%, t ≈ 2.01 for n = 50 and ≈ 1.98 for n = 100, against 1.96 for Z.

Interpreting it, the way the exam wants

  • ✔ "We are 95% confident that, in the population, the mean is between LB and UB."
  • ✔ If we drew many samples, about 95% of their intervals would contain µ.
  • ✘ "There is a 95% probability that µ is in [LB, UB]." µ is fixed: it is either inside a given interval or not.
  • ✘ "95% of the data are in the interval." The interval is about the mean, not about individuals.
  • ✘ "We are 95% confident that the sample mean is in…" You already know the sample mean exactly.

Sample-size rule: n > 50, use the formula. n < 30, only if the variable is normal; otherwise STOP, the interval is unreliable. An interval for a group of 28 should not be reported.

Writing the null and alternative hypotheses

You'll be able toturn any question into H0 and H1 and say whether the test is one- or two-tailed.

A test checks a claim about the population using a sample. Think of a trial: the defendant is presumed innocent (H0) and is declared guilty (H1) only if the evidence is strong enough.

H0, the null hypothesisH1, the alternative hypothesis
Status quo, state of nature, historical value: what we assumeThe claim, usually what the analyst wants to show
Always contains the equality: =, ≤ or ≥Never contains an equality: ≠, < or >
Innocent · not pregnant · vaccine has no effect · variables independentGuilty · pregnant · vaccine works · variables dependent
Question wordingH0H1Tail
"Is it equal to 400?", "has it changed?"µ = 400µ ≠ 400two-tailed (α/2 on each side)
"Has it increased above 46?"µ ≤ 46µ > 46right-tailed
"Has it decreased below 3?"µ ≥ 3µ < 3left-tailed

The tail is named after where H1 points. The expected value in H0 is written µ0 (or p0 for a proportion).

Hypotheses use µ, never x̄. "H0: x̄ = 5" is always a wrong MCQ option. You already know x̄, so there is nothing to test about it.

"x̄ = 4 ≠ 5, so no test is needed." Wrong: a sample can differ from µ by chance. That is the whole point of testing.

Lesson 9 · Chapter 3 part 1

Type I and Type II errors: what α really is

You'll be able todefine α and β without mixing them up.

Because we decide from a sample, we can be wrong in two ways. The course uses a pregnancy test: H0 = "Anna is not pregnant".

Decision ↓ / Reality →H0 true (not pregnant)H1 true (pregnant)
Do not reject H0✔ correct (1 − α)Type II error, false negative (β)
Reject H0Type I error, false positive or false alarm (α)✔ correct (1 − β, the power)
α = P(conclude H1 | H0 true)  ·  β = P(conclude H0 | H1 true)α is the significance level, chosen before the test (usually 5%). Power (1 − β) is not covered this semester.
  • Lowering α (fewer false alarms) raises β (more missed detections). It is a trade-off.
  • α does not shrink by itself when n grows. You choose it.
  • "Level of significance" (α) is not the same as "level of confidence" (1 − α).

Lesson 10 · Chapter 3 part 1

tSTAT, critical values and degrees of freedom

You'll be able toexplain how the old "bounds" method decides, and define degrees of freedom.

Wedding example: H0 says µ = 30. Our sample (n = 100) gives x̄ = 25 with s = 20. Is 25 "far" from 30? To judge, we measure the gap in units of standard error:

tSTAT = (x̄ − µ0) / (s/√n) = (25 − 30) / (20/√100) = −5 / 2 = −2.5

The critical values ±tα/2 are the bounds that leave α/2 = 2.5% in each tail. With 99 df, t0.025 ≈ 1.98 (about 2). Since −2.5 < −1.98, the observation lies outside the bounds, so we reject H0: at a 5% level the mean age is not 30.

  • Expected value: µ0 (from H0). Observed value: x̄ (from the sample).
  • Standard deviation of the sample s: spread of individuals. Standard deviation between samples s/√n: the standard error.

Degrees of freedom

The course's imperfect definition: "the quantity of information available above the minimum needed to obtain a result", or the number of values free to vary. If 5 ages must average 20 and you choose 4 of them freely, the 5th is forced. That gives 4 = n − 1 degrees of freedom.

  • One-sample t test: df = n − 1. Two independent samples: df = n − 2 (you need at least one person per group). So df is not always n − 1.
  • More df means more information, which is desirable. The t distribution then gets closer to the normal distribution.

You won't read statistical tables in the exam: SPSS gives tSTAT and the p-value (next lesson).

Lesson 11 · Chapter 3 part 1

The p-value: the one number you compare with α

You'll be able todefine the p-value in your own words, decide with it, and word the conclusion.
Definition: the p-value is the probability, if H0 is true, of getting a sample result as extreme or more extreme than the one we observed. Geometrically, it is the area beyond the observed value, so it is always between 0 and 1.

If that probability is tiny, our sample would be very surprising in a world where H0 is true. Either we drew a freakishly unrepresentative sample (unlikely), or H0 is wrong. We bet on the second and reject H0.

p-value = α = 0.05

Large-sample curve (normal). The dashed lines are the 5% critical values. Notice that tSTAT beyond the critical line and p < α always happen together: they are the same decision.

p-value < α → reject H0  ·  p-value ≥ α → fail to reject H0

Wording the conclusion (step 8)

  • Reject: "At a 5% level of significance, there is enough statistical evidence to reject H0. The mean price is not equal to 5€."
  • Fail to reject: "There is not enough statistical evidence, at a 5% level, to reject H0. The mean price might be equal to 5€."
  • Never say "H0 is true", "we accept H0" or "we proved H1".
p-valuePlain-language label
> 10%not significant
5–10%not very significant
1–5%significant

"The p-value is the probability that H0 (or H1) is true" or "the likelihood of H0": always wrong. Also compare with the α the question gives you: p = 0.03 is significant at 5% but not at 1%.

The 8 steps of a test

  1. Set up H0 and H1.
  2. What are we working on? (mean, proportion, one or several groups…)
  3. Choose the test.
  4. Check the assumptions (sample size, normality…).
  5. Choose α.
  6. Read the p-value and compare it with α.
  7. Decide: reject or fail to reject H0.
  8. State the conclusion in context.

Lesson 12 · Chapter 3 part 2

Choosing the test: the four questions

You'll be able toname the right test (with H0 and H1) for any situation. The exam expects the whole tree.
  1. How many groups or samples? One (compare with a target). One sample with two variables (correlation or independence). Two. Three or more.
  2. What are we working on? A mean (or median), a dispersion, a proportion, a distribution, a correlation, or independence.
  3. Independent or paired? Only asked with 2+ groups. Paired means each value in one group is matched with one in the other: the same person before/after, twins, couples, the same product in two countries.
  4. Assumptions met? n > 50 (both groups for two-sample tests)? Normality? Equal variances? If not, use the non-parametric backup.

Consistency check: the CI of the difference excludes 0 exactly when the two-sided p < 0.05. If it contains 0, p ≥ 0.05.

"We are 95% confident µ is between 0.0803 and 0.3496." Wrong, because that is the difference. Also wrong: "everyone spends 5.215 ± 1.876".

Check n first. If N = 18 (and normality is unknown), say the test should not be used.

Two independent samples: Levene first, then line 1 or line 2

You'll be able toread the Independent Samples Test table in the right order and explain why.

Question: do Netflix subscribers and non-subscribers have the same mean income? H0: µyes = µno, H1: µyes ≠ µno. Both groups must have more than 50 cases. If either has fewer, the test is not relevant.

SPSS runs three tests at once:

  1. Levene's test for equality of variances. H0: the two groups have the same dispersion.
  2. A t test assuming equal variances (line 1).
  3. A Welch test not assuming equal variances (line 2).
